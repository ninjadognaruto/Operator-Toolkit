# Microsoft Product Reaction Cards — full 118-word list + category subsets

The canonical desirability vocabulary, built by Joey Benedek and Trish Miner at Microsoft (2002). The full list is **118 words, ~60% positive / 40% negative or neutral**. The asymmetry is intentional — to counteract social-desirability bias in face-to-face usability lab settings.

**Method**: from the relevant subset, force-pick **exactly 5 cards** that describe the visceral feel of the design. No more, no less. No qualifiers. The forcing function is the whole point.

**Order matters**: the first card picked is the dominant signal. Number the picks 1-5 in order of how strongly they fit.

## How to apply

1. **Read Step 0 context.** Identify the category.
2. **Pick the matching subset.** If category is ambiguous, ask the user or default to Generic.
3. **From that subset, force-pick exactly 5 cards** that describe the visceral feel.
4. **Number them 1-5 in order of dominance.** First pick is the strongest signal.
5. **For each card, give a 1-line concrete reason.** Cite a specific element on the page (color, layout, copy, image) — not vague vibes.

### Example output

```
Reaction Cards picked from B2B SaaS / Productivity subset:

▢ professional (1)  — clean grid, restrained accent color use
▢ dense (2)         — heavy text-to-whitespace ratio in the upper third
▢ trustworthy (3)   — financial logos in the footer carry weight
▢ dated (4)         — Helvetica + 2010s gradients on the hero CTA
▢ rigid (5)         — no playful elements; corporate stiffness throughout
```

The pattern (3 positive + 2 negative, with "dated" and "rigid" as the dissonance) is the diagnostic. The brand reads as professional and trustworthy *but feels old and stiff*. That's the headline.

---

## Why this works

- **Forced choice prevents hedging.** "Pick 5" isn't "describe how it feels."
- **60/40 positive-negative balance** ensures the negative options are present and easy to pick.
- **Dominance ordering** distinguishes the primary signal from supporting cues.
- **Category-tuned subsets** prevent applying "rigid" / "efficient" to a beauty product, or "sensual" / "expressive" to a fintech.
- **Concrete reasons** anchor each pick to a specific visual element, preventing vibes-based critique.
