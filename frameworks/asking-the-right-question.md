# Asking the Right Question

Great leaders don't have the best answers. They ask the question that changes the room.

---

## Why It Matters

Every question does two things: it evokes the right *answer* and it evokes the right *thinking*. The best questions anchor discussion at the right altitude — not too deep, not too high — and they drive both creativity and exhaustiveness in the response. Think of a conversation as a tree: your question determines which branch you explore.

Three places this matters most: nudging a team toward a decision, extracting real insight from customers, and getting signal from interviews.

---

## The Core Toolkit

### Go Deep: 5 Whys

Keep asking "why" until you hit bedrock. In customer conversations, this surfaces the real push, pull, habits, and anxiety behind behavior. In product or engineering, it drives first-principle thinking. Most people stop at the first plausible answer. Don't.

### Eigen-Questions (Shishir Mehrotra)

An eigen-question is the one question whose answer determines the answer to all other questions. If you find it, you skip the noise. In any meeting or decision, ask yourself: *What is the single question that, once answered, makes everything else fall into place?* This is the intellectual cousin of Rahul Vohra's Single Decisive Reason — the SDR for questions.

### Inversion

Don't ask "what should we do?" Ask "what would guarantee failure?" Don't ask "what do I want?" Ask "what will I regret NOT having?" Flipping the question exposes blind spots that forward-looking questions miss.

### Hypothetical Situations

Force the other person to simulate a scenario that reveals what you actually need to know, without asking directly.

Examples from reference calls: *"Imagine you see his name pop up on your caller ID. What is he calling about and how do you feel seeing his name?"* or *"If I were to pair him with someone else, what complementary skills should that person have?"*

These bypass rehearsed answers and tap into instinct.

### Contrast Gives Clarity

Instead of accepting an answer at face value, introduce the opposite: *"Why not do X instead?"* This forces the person to articulate the real reasoning, not the default one.

### Understanding Context Behind the Answer

Ask about the environment, the people present, the situation. *Who was with you when you made that decision? What was happening at the time?* Context reveals the forces acting on a decision — push, pull, habit, anxiety — far better than the decision itself.

### Summarize and Reflect Back

Restating what someone said — accurately — does two things. It verifies your understanding. And it signals acceptance, which opens people up to share more. This is trust-building through listening.

### Follow the Energy

Pay attention to *how* people say things, not just what they say. When someone's energy shifts — voice lifts, pace changes, they lean in — dig there. That's where the real story lives.

### Play Dumb

Deliberately under-assume to get the other person to explain more. Works especially well with experts who will over-explain when they think you don't know. You get richer, less filtered information.

## Advanced Techniques (From Research)

### SPIN Selling (Neil Rackham)

Four question types, used in sequence, that move a buyer from status quo to action. Situation questions establish context. Problem questions surface pain. Implication questions make the pain feel bigger by exploring consequences. Need-Payoff questions shift focus to the value of solving it. The key insight: most salespeople ask too many Situation questions and not enough Implication questions. Implication is where urgency is built.

*"What happens to your team when that system goes down?" (Implication) is 10x more powerful than "How often does your system go down?" (Problem).*

### Calibrated Questions (Chris Voss)

From hostage negotiation. Open-ended questions starting with "how" and "what" give the other party the illusion of control while steering outcomes toward yours. *"How am I supposed to do that?"* is Voss's signature move — it makes the counterparty solve your problem for you. Avoid "why" (triggers defensiveness), "can/is/are/do/does" (invite one-word answers). The goal is to get the other side working on your behalf without realizing it.

### Humble Inquiry (Edgar Schein)

Ask questions where you genuinely don't know the answer. Not Socratic questioning (where you're leading), not diagnostic questioning (where you're categorizing) — actual curiosity. Schein's argument: most leaders default to "telling" because it feels faster, but telling destroys psychological safety. Humble Inquiry is the antidote. It requires temporarily subordinating your status and genuinely being interested in the other person's world.

Best for: leadership conversations where trust is thin, upward communication, any situation where someone isn't sharing what they actually think.

### Clean Language (David Grove)

Use the person's exact words back to them with minimal presupposition. Only ~12 questions exist in the framework: *"What kind of [their word]?" "Is there anything else about [their word]?" "Where is [their word]?" "That's like what?"* The "cleanness" comes from refusing to insert your assumptions, metaphors, or framing. The person discovers their own meaning.

Originally developed for trauma therapy, but powerful in coaching and customer research. When you catch yourself rephrasing what someone said in your own words, you're contaminating their thinking. Clean Language prevents that.

### Laddering (Means-End Chain Analysis)

Repeated "why is that important to you?" questions climb from attributes → consequences → personal values. A customer says they want a fast app (attribute). Why? Because they don't want to waste time (functional consequence). Why does that matter? Because they feel behind at work (emotional consequence). Why does that matter? Because competence is core to their identity (terminal value).

This is the 5 Whys applied specifically to consumer motivation. It reveals the value hierarchy behind any stated preference. Powerful for brand positioning — you learn which level to pitch at.

### The Miracle Question (Solution-Focused Brief Therapy)

*"Suppose tonight while you're asleep, a miracle happens and the problem is completely solved. You don't know it happened because you were asleep. What's the first thing you'd notice different tomorrow morning?"*

Forces concrete visualization of the desired end state without getting bogged down in how to get there. The specificity of "first thing you'd notice" grounds it — you get observable behaviors, not abstract wishes. Developed by Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg. Useful in coaching, goal-setting, and any conversation where someone is stuck describing what they actually want.

### The Dan Sullivan Question (Strategic Coach)

*"If we were having this discussion three years from today, and you were looking back over those three years, what has to have happened in your life — personally and professionally — for you to feel happy with your progress?"*

Time-travel forces clarity. The three-year horizon is long enough to think big, short enough to feel real. The "happy with your progress" framing avoids perfectionism. Use it in planning conversations, coaching, or with yourself.

### Appreciative Inquiry (David Cooperrider)

Most problem-solving starts with "what's broken?" Appreciative Inquiry starts with "what's working?" The 5D process: Define (choose a positive focus) → Discover (*"Tell me about a time you were most alive in this work"*) → Dream (envision the best possible future) → Design (craft the desired state) → Deliver (act).

The core insight — called the Simultaneity Principle — is that the question itself begins to create change the moment it's asked. If you ask a team "why do we keep failing?" you get defensiveness. If you ask "when were we at our best, and what was true about that moment?" you get energy and usable data. The question *is* the intervention.

### Motivational Interviewing — OARS (Miller & Rollnick)

Open-ended questions → Affirmations → Reflections → Summaries. Designed to evoke "change talk" — the person's own arguments for change — rather than the interviewer arguing for it. Key techniques: readiness scaling (*"On a 1-10 scale, how ready are you to make this change? Why not lower?"*), query extremes (*"What's the worst that could happen if nothing changes?"*), and looking back/forward (*"How was this different a year ago?"*).

Originally for addiction treatment, but the mechanics apply anywhere someone is ambivalent. The principle: people resist being told to change, but they'll talk themselves into it if you ask the right questions.

### Cognitive Interview Technique (Geiselman & Fisher)

Reinstate the full sensory context of a past event before asking about it. *"Take yourself back to that moment. What did you see? What sounds were around you? How were you feeling?"* Then ask your questions. Memory is state-dependent — the more context cues you activate, the more accurate and detailed the recall.

Developed for law enforcement eyewitness interviews, but directly applicable to customer research when you need precise behavioral recall, not reconstructed narratives.

### No-7 Rating (Tim Ferriss)

When rating anything on a 1-10 scale, ban the number 7. Force a 6 or an 8. Seven is where people hide when they're unsure, polite, or avoiding commitment. Eliminating it forces a directional call. Use it in hiring rubrics, opportunity evaluation, prioritization — anywhere a lukewarm middle is masking real signal.

### Backcasting (John B. Robinson)

Start with a concrete future state. Work backwards to identify what must be true at each stage to get there. Unlike forecasting (which projects the present forward), backcasting asks *"What conditions must exist for this future to happen?"* at every step. The questions get more specific as you approach the present, until you land on *"What do we need to do this quarter?"*

Use for long-term strategy, product vision, or any time you need to reverse-engineer a path.

## Asking Customers the Right Questions

The rules change with customers because what people *say* and what they *do* are often different things. Here's the hierarchy from least to most reliable:

1. **How they might act in the future** — least accurate ("Would you pay for this?" is almost useless)
2. **How they acted in the past (their version)** — better, but filtered through memory and identity
3. **How they would act right now** — more grounded, closer to behavior
4. **How they acted in the past (recorded version)** — most accurate, because it's observed, not recalled

The core principle: ask about their life, not your idea. Ask about specifics in the past, not hypotheticals in the future. Look for workarounds — people hacking existing solutions to get what they need is the strongest signal of latent demand (Nikita Bier's test).

The JTBD interview frame is powerful here: you're trying to uncover the push away from the current solution, the pull toward a new one, the habits that create inertia, and the anxiety about switching. Every customer question should be designed to surface one of those four forces.

For deeper motivation, use Laddering to climb from stated preferences to terminal values. For precise behavioral recall, use the Cognitive Interview Technique to reinstate sensory context before asking what happened.

## In Interviews and Reference Checks

The McKinsey behavioral method applies: ask about *their* specific contribution, not the team's. Use "how" and "what" — "why" is a leading question that lets people rationalize after the fact. Go deeper into exact meetings and conversations.

Great reference call questions use the hypothetical and contrast techniques together. *"Think about all the other product people you've ever worked with. On a scale of 0 to 100, where does this person fall?"* forces calibration. *"I look to hire people who demonstrate three values: X, Y, Z. Which is this person strongest in? Weakest in? Why?"* forces ranking, which is always more revealing than rating.

---

## In Negotiations

Lead with Calibrated Questions (Voss): "how" and "what" questions that make the other side solve your constraints for you. Layer in Contrast ("why not do X?") to surface their real boundaries. Use Summarize and Reflect Back to build tactical empathy — when the other side says "that's right," you've earned trust. Never ask "why" in a negotiation; it sounds accusatory.

---

## In Coaching and 1:1s

Start with Humble Inquiry (Schein) — genuine curiosity, not diagnostic. Use Clean Language to avoid contaminating their thinking. If they're stuck, deploy the Miracle Question or the Dan Sullivan Question to bypass the present and visualize the end state. If they're ambivalent, use OARS (Motivational Interviewing) to let them talk themselves into change.

---

## In Strategy and Team Settings

Use "How Might We" to convert insights into actionable brainstorming prompts. Use Appreciative Inquiry when the team is stuck in problem-mode and needs to rediscover what works. Use Backcasting when you need to reverse-engineer a path from vision to action. Use the No-7 Rating to force honest prioritization in any scoring exercise.

---

## The Meta-Principle

Before reaching for a technique, ask yourself two things:

**What do I actually need to learn?** Most bad questions fail here. They're asked out of habit or politeness, not because the answer would change a decision.

**At what altitude should this conversation happen?** Too high and you get platitudes. Too deep and you lose the forest. The best question operates at exactly the level where the answer is actionable.

Then pick the right tool:

| Need | Technique |
|------|-----------|
| Get to root cause | 5 Whys |
| Find the one question that matters | Eigen-question |
| Expose blind spots | Inversion |
| Bypass rehearsed answers | Hypothetical situations |
| Force real reasoning | Contrast |
| Build trust while listening | Summarize & Reflect Back |
| Find what someone really cares about | Follow the Energy |
| Get unfiltered information | Play Dumb, Humble Inquiry |
| Build urgency in a buyer | SPIN (Implication questions) |
| Make the other side solve your problem | Calibrated Questions (Voss) |
| Avoid contaminating their thinking | Clean Language |
| Climb from preferences to values | Laddering |
| Open up solution space | "How Might We" |
| Visualize the desired end state | Miracle Question, Dan Sullivan Question |
| Shift from problems to strengths | Appreciative Inquiry |
| Evoke self-driven change | OARS (Motivational Interviewing) |
| Get accurate behavioral recall | Cognitive Interview |
| Force honest prioritization | No-7 Rating |
| Reverse-engineer from vision | Backcasting |
