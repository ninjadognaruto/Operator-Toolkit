# Influence — Criteria Reference

Full reference for the 12 interpersonal influence criteria. Critical and High criteria get deep dives with mechanisms, sub-components, named examples, originator quotes, and diagnostic questions. Medium criteria get standard treatment.

---

## How to read this reference

Each criterion follows this structure:

- **Definition** — what this property measures
- **Why it matters** — the mechanism; how and why this affects influence quality
- **Impact tier** — Critical (5), High (4), or Medium (3)
- **Best direction** — higher is better unless noted
- **Good example / Poor example** — concrete, specific illustrations
- **Research basis** — source or citation

Critical and High criteria also include:
- **Sub-components** — named parts of the framework
- **Originator quotes** — direct language from the source
- **Connection to other criteria** — how this interacts with the rest
- **Diagnostic questions** — what to ask when evaluating

# Category 1: Preparation

## Criterion 1 — Demand-Side Diagnosis

**Definition:** Depth of understanding of the other person's world — their struggling moment, what progress they're trying to make, their constraints, and what they actually care about (functional, emotional, social).

**Why it matters:** You cannot influence someone whose world you don't understand. Every failed pitch, stalled deal, and lost hire traces back to the same root cause: the influencer was operating from their own frame, not the other person's. Moesta's demand-side framework inverts the default — instead of "how do I sell this?" you ask "what progress are they trying to make, and what are they willing to pay for it?" Carnegie's version: "The only way on earth to influence other people is to talk about what they want and show them how to get it." Cohen/Bradford: "Diagnose the world of the other person."

**Impact tier:** Critical (5/5)

**Best direction:** Higher is better. The deeper your diagnosis, the more precisely you can target every other criterion.

### Sub-components

**The Struggling Moment (Moesta)**
Ground zero for all demand. The situational context where someone says "I need something else because what I'm doing now isn't working." Key properties:
- It builds over time (weeks, months, sometimes years)
- There's usually a **crystallization moment** — the straw that breaks the camel's back. A comment from someone, a failure, an embarrassment.
- Before the struggling moment, they can't "see" alternatives — their head is full of other priorities.

**Three Motivation Categories (Moesta)**
- **Functional:** How does this practically improve their situation? What metrics are they measured on?
- **Emotional:** How does this make them feel? What internal anxiety or aspiration drives them?
- **Social:** How will others perceive them? What does saying yes (or no) signal to their peers, boss, board?

Most people only diagnose functional. The emotional and social layers are where influence actually happens.

**Hiring and Firing (Moesta)**
Every decision is a hire (new solution) and a fire (old one). Understanding what they're firing — and what loss that represents — is as important as what they're hiring. The firing often has emotional weight that the hiring doesn't: sunk cost, identity, relationships with people who built the old thing.

**Non-Consumption as Competition (Christensen/Moesta)**
Their real alternative is often not a competitor product. McDonald's milkshakes competed against bagels, bananas, and boredom — not other milkshakes. Understanding the customer's actual alternatives (including doing nothing) reshapes your positioning.

**The Timeline Interview (Moesta)**
Forensic reconstruction of a switching decision. Walk them backward from the moment of action:
- When did you first notice the problem?
- When did you start looking at alternatives?
- When did you make the decision? What happened that day?
- What almost stopped you?

**Frozen-in-time technique:** Press for vivid, specific moments. "I wasn't sleeping well" → "What specifically woke you up?" → "The spring in the mattress dug into my back." Specificity reveals the emotional weight and urgency.

### Named examples

**The Mattress (Moesta's canonical case):** A customer "impulsively" bought a mattress at Costco. Timeline interview revealed: he'd wanted to replace it for over a year. The real push was a friend's comment — "You're too old to still be sleeping on a hand-me-down mattress" (identity/social trigger). The job wasn't "get better sleep" but "stop feeling like I'm failing at basic adulting." Costco's return policy resolved anxiety. His wife being present resolved the "need to discuss" habit. All four forces aligned that Saturday morning. An "impulse purchase" is never impulse.

**Autobooks:** Sales demo wasn't converting. JTBD interviews revealed the demo didn't match prospects' hiring criteria at different stages of their decision journey. Solution: three separate demos, each aligned to different customer jobs and timeline stages. Result: 3× lead conversion, 50% reduction in time to close, 5× revenue growth.

### Originator quotes

> "Don't sell. Help them make progress." — Bob Moesta
>
> "The only way on earth to influence other people is to talk about what they want and show them how to get it." — Dale Carnegie
>
> "Assume all are potential allies. Clarify your goals and priorities. Diagnose the world of the other person." — Cohen & Bradford

### Diagnostic questions

- Can you describe their struggling moment in vivid, specific detail — not abstract categories?
- Do you know their hiring criteria across all three layers (functional, emotional, social)?
- What are they firing by saying yes to you, and what loss does that represent?
- What is their actual alternative if they say no? (Not your competitor — their real fallback, including doing nothing.)
- Have you pressed for specificity, or are you working from assumptions?

### Connection to other criteria

Feeds directly into Force Field Mapping (#8) — you can't map forces without knowing the terrain. Feeds into Pre-Suasion (#4) — you can't prime them for the right frame without knowing what frame they're in. Feeds into Identity & Unity (#9) — you can't connect to their identity if you don't know how they see themselves. Without this diagnosis, every other criterion is a technique deployed blind.

### Good example
Before pitching an investor, you reconstruct their portfolio thesis, recent losses, what they're publicly saying they want, and what pressures they face from LPs. You frame your pitch around their progress, not your features.

### Poor example
You walk in with your standard deck, talk about your product, and assume they care about the same things you do.

### Research basis
Bob Moesta & Chris Spiek (The Re-Wired Group); *Demand-Side Sales 101* (Moesta & Engle); *Learning to Build* (Moesta, 2022); *Competing Against Luck* (Christensen, Hall, Dillon, Duncan); Dale Carnegie — *How to Win Friends and Influence People*; Cohen & Bradford — *Influence Without Authority*.

## Criterion 4 — Pre-Suasion & Framing

**Definition:** Deliberate shaping of the context, environment, and attention direction before delivering the actual message. Includes priming, anchoring, loss framing, and creating "privileged moments" that make the audience receptive.

**Why it matters:** Cialdini's central insight — what you do *before* the message determines whether the message lands. Attention is not just a prerequisite for persuasion; it IS the first act of persuasion. Kahneman showed that 96% of thinking is automatic and unconscious (System 1). Effective pre-suasion targets System 1: concrete images, emotional stories, familiar situations, simple language. If you want people to act, reach them through System 1 first.

**Impact tier:** High (4/5)

**Best direction:** Higher is better.

### Sub-components

**Privileged Moments (Cialdini)**
Windows of receptivity created by priming. The moment immediately before a request when the audience's psychological state can be influenced to be more receptive.
- "Do you consider yourself a helpful person?" → 77% agreed to a survey (vs. 29% cold)
- "Are you adventurous?" → dramatically increases willingness to try new products
- Three trigger types: **words** you say, **situations** you create, **images/environments** you present

**Anchoring (HBS Negotiation)**
The first number on the table sticks. Most final deals land close to the first number.
- Anchor first if you have more information
- Anchor high — leave room to concede
- Use a single upper benchmark; don't muddy it with alternatives

**Loss Framing (Kahneman)**
"You'll lose X" outperforms "You'll gain X" by approximately 2×. Loss aversion is one of the most robust findings in behavioral economics.
- Frame proposals around what they'll lose by not acting
- "90% survival rate" vs. "10% mortality rate" — same fact, different response
- The endowment effect: people overvalue what they already have

**System 1 Targeting (Kahneman)**
System 1 reacts to the frame, not the content. It builds judgment on what's right in front of it and systematically ignores what's absent.
- Concrete beats abstract: "We cut churn by 12%" beats "we improved retention metrics"
- Stories beat data: a single vivid case outweighs a statistical summary for changing minds
- Simple language beats complex: reduces cognitive load, increases acceptance

### Named examples

**Furniture Store Study:** An online furniture store sent half of visitors to a landing page with fluffy cloud wallpaper, the other half to a page with coins. Those who saw clouds rated comfort as most important and purchased comfortable furniture. Those who saw coins prioritized price. The background image — which nobody consciously noticed — primed the decision criteria.

**The "Helpful Person" Study:** Researchers asked "Do you consider yourself a helpful person?" before requesting survey participation. 77% agreed, vs. 29% when asked directly. The question activated the self-concept of helpfulness, creating a privileged moment.

**Machiavelli's Rule (from HBS):** Good news: little by little. Bad news: all at once. Drip positive information to extend the emotional uplift. Consolidate negative information to limit the damage window.

### Originator quotes

> "The best persuaders become the best through pre-suasion — the process of arranging for recipients to be receptive to a message before they encounter it." — Robert Cialdini
>
> "What is salient is important. What is focal is causal." — Daniel Kahneman

### Diagnostic questions

- What have you done to prime their attention before making your request?
- Is your framing oriented around loss (what they'll miss) or gain (what they'll get)?
- Are you targeting System 1 (stories, images, emotion) or System 2 (data, analysis)?
- Did you anchor the conversation, or did they?
- What's the "privileged moment" you're creating — what question or context primes them for your ask?

### Connection to other criteria

Depends on Demand-Side Diagnosis (#1) — you can't frame effectively without knowing what frame they're in. Reinforces Information Control (#11) — timing and sequence of reveals is a form of pre-suasion. Works in tandem with Identity & Unity (#9) — priming their self-concept before the ask.

### Good example
Before asking your team to take on an ambitious Q2 goal, you spend two weeks sharing customer stories about the problem you'll solve. By the time you present the goal, they're already emotionally committed to the cause.

### Poor example
You drop the ambitious goal in a Monday standup with no setup. It feels like a mandate from above.

### Research basis
Robert Cialdini — *Pre-Suasion* (2016); Daniel Kahneman — *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011); HBS Negotiation course notes (anchoring, loss framing, Machiavelli's rule).

## Criterion 8 — Force Field Mapping

**Definition:** Explicit diagnosis of all four forces acting on the other person's decision: push of the current situation, pull of the new solution, anxiety of change, and habit of the present. Influence strategy targets the binding constraint — not all forces equally.

**Why it matters:** Moesta's Four Forces framework is the tactical planning layer of influence. It explains *why* people switch (or don't), not what your product does. The switching equation: **If (Push + Pull) > (Anxiety + Habit) → they act.** Both motivating forces are required — push without pull is frustration with nowhere to go; pull without push means they can't justify the change. Most influence attempts fail by adding more pull (more features, more arguments, more benefits) when the real blocker is unaddressed anxiety or unbroken habit.

**Impact tier:** High (4/5)

**Best direction:** Higher is better — the more precisely you've mapped all four forces, the more targeted your intervention.

### Sub-components

**Push of the Current Situation**
Frustration, pain, or inadequacy of the status quo. Not about your proposal — about why what they're doing now isn't working. The struggling moment is the source of push.

**Pull of the New Solution**
The magnetism of a better future. What they imagine life looks like after switching. The vision of progress — not features, but outcomes.

**Anxiety of the New**
Fear and uncertainty about the switch itself. Will it work? What if it's worse? Learning curve, unproven product, social risk ("what if I chose wrong?"). This is where most deals die.

**Habit of the Present**
Allegiance to the familiar. Comfort, inertia, sunk costs, "the way we've always done it." Even when the current solution is objectively bad, habit keeps people locked in.

### Connection to Fogg's B=MAP

Push/Pull = Motivation. Anxiety/Habit = inverse of Ability. The prompt (your ask) only works when motivation × ability crosses the threshold. Four Forces gives you the SPECIFIC forces to diagnose, not just the abstract equation.

### Diagnostic questions

- What is pushing them away from their current situation? Is the push strong enough, or are they merely uncomfortable (not yet in pain)?
- What is pulling them toward your proposal? Is it features, or is it a vision of progress they can feel?
- What specific anxiety is blocking the decision? Name it — not "risk" in general, but the specific fear.
- What habit are they giving up? What comfort, routine, or sunk cost makes staying feel safe?
- Which force is the binding constraint? Are you targeting it, or are you adding more pull to a problem that's actually about anxiety?

### Good example
You map out why your VP of Eng hasn't approved the platform migration: push exists (current system breaks monthly), pull exists (new system demo impressed them), but anxiety is the blocker (fear of 3 months of instability during migration). You design a phased rollback plan that directly resolves the anxiety.

### Poor example
You keep making the case for the new platform (adding pull) when the real blocker is anxiety about the transition. More pull doesn't overcome unaddressed anxiety.

### Research basis
Bob Moesta & Chris Spiek (The Re-Wired Group); *Demand-Side Sales 101*; BJ Fogg — *Tiny Habits*, B=MAP behavior model.

# Category 2: Connection

## Criterion 2 — Tactical Empathy

**Definition:** Active demonstration that you understand the other person's emotional state and perspective — through labeling, mirroring, and summarizing — such that they feel deeply heard before you ask for anything.

**Why it matters:** Emotional barriers must be cleared before rational engagement is possible. When someone feels heard, their amygdala deactivates and they shift from fight-or-flight to problem-solving mode. This isn't "being nice" — it's a deliberate technique that creates psychological safety as a precondition for influence. Voss developed this through decades of FBI hostage negotiation, where the stakes for getting empathy wrong were literal life and death.

**Impact tier:** Critical (5/5)

**Best direction:** Higher is better. The deeper the demonstrated empathy, the more trust you build and the more receptive they become.

### Sub-components

**Mirroring (Voss)**
Repeat the last 1-3 words they said, with a slightly rising intonation. This signals attention, forces elaboration, and creates rapport without revealing your position.
- "We're not sure this is the right time." → "Not the right time?"
- The pause after mirroring is critical — let them fill the silence.

**Labeling (Voss)**
Name the emotion you detect. Always starts with "It seems like..." / "It sounds like..." / "It looks like..." Never "I think you feel..." — that makes it about you and triggers defensiveness.
- "It seems like you're worried about the transition timeline."
- "It sounds like you've been burned by promises like this before."
- Labels work on both negative emotions (defusing) and positive emotions (reinforcing).

**Summarizing (Voss + MI)**
Combine a paraphrase of their position with a label of their emotion. The gold standard: a summary so accurate the only possible response is "That's right."
- "That's right" = turning point. They feel fully understood. The door to influence opens.
- "You're right" = danger signal. It means "go away, I'm done talking." These are opposite signals.

**OARS (Miller & Rollnick — Motivational Interviewing)**
The clinical version of the same mechanism:
- **Open questions** — draw out their perspective without leading
- **Affirmations** — acknowledge strengths, efforts, past successes (builds hope and agency)
- **Reflections** — repeat, rephrase, or offer a deeper guess about what they're communicating
- **Summaries** — collect and reflect back multiple themes

**The "That's Right" Moment (Voss)**
The culmination of tactical empathy. When your summary is so accurate that the only possible response is "That's right," you've created the turning point. They feel fully understood, their guard drops, and the conversation shifts from defense to collaboration.

### Named examples

**FBI Bank Robbery Negotiation (Voss):** In a bank robbery standoff, Voss spent hours doing nothing but labeling the robber's emotions — fear of going to prison, concern for his family, feeling trapped. No demands, no threats. Eventually the robber said "That's right" and walked out voluntarily. The empathy created the conditions for surrender that force never could have.

**MI in Addiction Treatment (Miller/Rollnick):** Counselors who used reflective listening and rolled with resistance had dramatically higher behavior change rates than those who confronted patients with evidence of their addiction. The mechanism: when people hear their own reasoning reflected back accurately, they resolve their own ambivalence.

### Originator quotes

> "Tactical empathy is understanding the feelings and mindset of another in the moment and also hearing what is behind those feelings so you increase your influence in all the moments that follow." — Chris Voss
>
> "Mirroring and labeling within the same sentence will show someone that you are very interested in and have empathy for them." — Chris Voss
>
> "Resistance is an indication that the health care provider should change strategies rather than argue." — Miller & Rollnick

### Diagnostic questions

- Have you labeled their emotional state out loud — or are you guessing silently?
- Did you get a "That's right" or a "You're right"? (Opposite signals.)
- Are you listening to respond, or listening to understand?
- Can you summarize their position so accurately they'd say "that's exactly it"?
- After mirroring or labeling, did you pause long enough for them to elaborate?

### Connection to other criteria

Works in tandem with Resistance Dissolution (#3) — empathy is the precondition, dissolution is the action. Reinforces Credibility (#7) — Aristotle's eunoia (goodwill toward the audience) is demonstrated through empathy. Depends on Demand-Side Diagnosis (#1) — the deeper your understanding, the more precise your labels.

### Good example
You label: "It sounds like you've been burned by vendors who overpromised before, and the last thing you want is another one." They respond: "That's right." Now the conversation can begin.

### Poor example
You acknowledge their concern with "I understand" (which signals nothing) and immediately pivot to your pitch. They feel processed, not heard.

### Research basis
Chris Voss — *Never Split the Difference*; Miller & Rollnick — *Motivational Interviewing* (4th ed.); Dale Carnegie — *How to Win Friends and Influence People*.

## Criterion 7 — Credibility Positioning

**Definition:** Establishing the right to be heard before attempting to influence — through demonstrated expertise (phronesis), moral character (arete), and visible goodwill toward the other person (eunoia).

**Why it matters:** Aristotle identified this 2,400 years ago and it still holds: "Character may almost be called the most effective means of persuasion." Without credibility, no technique works. With it, even simple requests carry weight. Cialdini's Authority principle operates on the same mechanism — people defer to those they perceive as knowledgeable and trustworthy.

**Impact tier:** High (4/5)

**Best direction:** Higher is better.

### Sub-components

**Phronesis (Aristotle) — Sound Reasoning / Expertise**
Not claimed authority but demonstrated pattern recognition. Tell a story that shows you've seen this situation before. Cite specific cases. Describe two approaches — one that worked and one that didn't — and what made the difference. This is expertise in action, not on a resume.

**Arete (Aristotle) — Moral Character**
Consistency, honesty, and fairness. Admit what you don't know — the willingness to say "I don't know" paradoxically increases credibility. Acknowledge the strength of the other side's position before presenting yours. Show you've considered their interests, not just your own.

**Eunoia (Aristotle) — Goodwill Toward the Audience**
Visible evidence that you're acting in their interest, not just yours. This is where credibility connects to Tactical Empathy (#2) — demonstrating understanding IS demonstrating goodwill.

**Authority Signaling (Cialdini)**
People respect expertise, credentials, and experience — but how you signal matters. Demonstrating authority through action (solving a problem, sharing an insight) is more powerful than claiming it (listing credentials).

### Diagnostic questions

- Are you demonstrating expertise through pattern recognition and stories, or claiming it through credentials?
- Have you admitted what you don't know?
- Have you acknowledged the other side's position before presenting yours?
- Is there visible evidence that you're acting in their interest?
- Would they describe you as credible if asked by a third party?

### Good example
When advising a founder on a pivot, you open by describing two similar pivots you've seen — one that worked and one that didn't — and what made the difference. You're not claiming authority; you're demonstrating pattern recognition.

### Poor example
You lead with your resume, drop the names of companies you've advised, and launch into recommendations. They feel talked at, not counseled.

### Research basis
Aristotle — *Rhetoric* (ethos: phronesis, arete, eunoia); Robert Cialdini — *Influence* (authority principle); HBS Negotiation (credibility in negotiation context).

# Category 3: Execution

## Criterion 5 — Calibrated Questions

**Definition:** Use of open-ended "how" and "what" questions that extract information, give the other person the illusion of control, and force them to solve your problem for you — without triggering defensiveness.

**Why it matters:** Calibrated questions transform confrontation into collaboration. They say "no" without saying no, surface constraints you didn't know about, and make the other person invest in finding a solution. Voss calls "How am I supposed to do that?" the greatest calibrated question of all time — it conveys "no" while genuinely asking for help.

**Impact tier:** High (4/5)

**Best direction:** Higher is better.

### Sub-components

**The GOAT Question (Voss)**
"How am I supposed to do that?" — deploys when someone makes an unreasonable demand. Critical: your delivery must convey genuine curiosity, not sarcasm. You're actually asking for help.

**Illusion of Control (Voss)**
Calibrated questions give the other side the feeling that they're in charge — they're the one with the answers and power — while actually constraining their options. "What's going to happen if we can't solve this?" forces them to consider consequences without you making threats.

**Key Question Templates:**
- "What's the biggest challenge you face with this?" (extracts the struggling moment)
- "How would you like me to proceed?" (gives control while you gather information)
- "What about this is important to you?" (uncovers real interests behind positions)
- "How can I help make this work for everyone?" (reframes from adversarial to collaborative)
- "What happens if we do nothing?" (forces them to articulate the cost of inaction)

**Avoid closed questions:** "Can," "is," "are," "do," "does" — these get yes/no answers and kill dialogue. Always "how" or "what."

**Connection to MI (Miller/Rollnick)**
The OARS "Open questions" component operates on the same principle. Open questions draw out the person's own reasoning for change, which is more persuasive than any argument you could make. People believe their own conclusions more than your arguments.

### Diagnostic questions

- Are you asking "how/what" questions, or making statements and demands?
- When they push back, do you argue or ask a question?
- Have you deployed "How am I supposed to do that?" when facing an unreasonable demand?
- Are your questions genuinely open, or are they leading questions disguised as open ones?

### Good example
Your client demands a 50% discount. Instead of saying no, you ask: "How am I supposed to do that and still deliver the quality you need?" They start problem-solving with you instead of against you.

### Poor example
You respond to the discount demand with "That's not possible" or counter with your own number. Now it's positional bargaining.

### Research basis
Chris Voss — *Never Split the Difference*; The Black Swan Group — negotiation training materials; Miller & Rollnick — *Motivational Interviewing*.

## Criterion 6 — Reciprocity Architecture

**Definition:** Strategic creation of obligation through giving before asking — identifying what the other person values (their "currencies") and delivering it before making your request, so the exchange feels natural rather than transactional.

**Why it matters:** Cialdini's first principle — we feel obligated to return favors. But effective reciprocity isn't "I did you a favor, now you owe me." It's understanding what the other person values (Cohen/Bradford's currencies) and delivering in THAT currency, not yours, before the ask even comes.

**Impact tier:** High (4/5)

**Best direction:** Higher is better.

### Sub-components

**Cialdini's Reciprocity Principle**
Free samples, unexpected favors, shared insights — they all activate the same mechanism: obligation to return. The gift should be meaningful, personalized, and unexpected for maximum effect.

**Cohen/Bradford's Five Currencies**
Not everyone values the same things. The five currency types:
1. **Inspiration-related:** Vision, meaning, moral correctness, excellence. "This matters because..."
2. **Task-related:** Resources, assistance, information, direct help. "Let me handle that for you."
3. **Position-related:** Recognition, visibility, advancement, reputation. "I mentioned your work in the leadership review."
4. **Relationship-related:** Acceptance, inclusion, personal support, understanding. "I've got your back."
5. **Personal-related:** Gratitude, ownership, self-concept, comfort. "You're the right person for this."

The key insight: most people offer what THEY value, not what the other person values. Diagnosing their currency (from criterion #1) and delivering in that currency is the mechanism.

### Diagnostic questions

- What have you given before asking? Is it meaningful to THEM (not just to you)?
- Do you know which currency type they value most?
- Is the reciprocity natural and well-timed, or does it feel like a transaction?
- Have you invested in the relationship before this specific ask?

### Good example
Before asking a cross-functional peer to prioritize your project, you help them hit their own deadline first, publicly credit their team's work in a leadership review, and share a useful contact. When you make your ask, they're already inclined to say yes.

### Poor example
You make your request cold, with no prior deposits in the relationship. Or worse — you do favors and immediately say "so can you do this for me?" making the exchange feel transactional.

### Research basis
Robert Cialdini — *Influence* (reciprocity principle); Cohen & Bradford — *Influence Without Authority* (currencies of exchange model).

## Criterion 3 — Resistance Dissolution

**Definition:** Ability to surface and neutralize objections, anxiety, and habit before they calcify into "no" — without confrontation. Includes accusation audits, rolling with resistance, and reducing anxiety of the new.

**Why it matters:** Most influence attempts fail not because of insufficient motivation but because of unaddressed resistance. Moesta's framework shows that anxiety of the new and habit of the present are where most deals die — not lack of interest. Voss's accusation audit and MI's "rolling with resistance" both operate on the same principle: **name the resistance before it names you.** Direct confrontation increases resistance. Absorbing and redirecting it dissolves resistance.

**Impact tier:** Critical (5/5)

**Best direction:** Higher is better.

### Sub-components

**Accusation Audit (Voss)**
List every negative thing they could say about you or your proposal. Say it first, out loud, before they do. When you voice their objections, the accusations sound exaggerated, and they're compelled to correct you: "No, it's not that bad." You've flipped them from adversary to collaborator.

Structure: "You're probably thinking [worst thing]. You might feel like [second worst thing]. It may seem like [third worst thing]."

**Rolling with Resistance (Miller/Rollnick)**
Don't argue against objections. Reflect them back, amplified slightly:
- "You really enjoy the current setup. Changing seems like a lot of effort for uncertain payoff."
- "It sounds like the timing couldn't be worse."

The mechanism: when you agree with their resistance instead of fighting it, they often talk themselves out of it. Direct confrontation creates defensive reactions; rolling creates space for self-persuasion.

**Anxiety Reduction (Moesta)**
Anxiety is specific and addressable. Identify the specific fear and design a mechanism to neutralize it:
- Free trials, money-back guarantees, return policies (removes financial anxiety)
- Rollback plans, phased implementations (removes operational anxiety)
- Social proof, testimonials, case studies (removes social anxiety — "others like me did this")
- Demos, pilots, proofs of concept (removes performance anxiety — "will it actually work?")

**Habit Breaking (Moesta)**
Habit is the comfort of the familiar. You have to make the transition feel smaller than the gap:
- Migration tools, "bring your data" features (reduces switching friction)
- Side-by-side trials (lets them keep the old while testing the new)
- Switching incentives (makes the math obvious)
- "What's this costing you?" framing (makes the invisible cost of habit visible)

### Named examples

**Voss — High-Stakes Negotiation:** Before a tense business negotiation, Voss led with: "I know it's going to seem like we're trying to take advantage of you, and that we don't care about your situation, and that we're being greedy." The other side immediately said: "No, no, that's not what we think at all" — and the conversation opened up.

**MI — Addiction Counseling:** A counselor reflecting back: "You really enjoy smoking. It relaxes you and it's part of your routine." The patient, instead of defending the habit, begins volunteering reasons they might want to quit. The resistance dissolved because it wasn't being fought.

### Originator quotes

> "Performing an accusation audit in advance prepares you to head off negative dynamics before they take root." — Chris Voss
>
> "Resistance is not opposition to be overcome but information about what the person needs." — Miller & Rollnick

### Diagnostic questions

- Have you run an accusation audit — listed every objection they could raise?
- What specific anxiety is blocking the decision? (Not general "risk" — the specific fear.)
- What habit are they giving up by saying yes? Have you made the transition feel safe?
- Are you arguing against their resistance, or absorbing it?
- If you've been pushing and they haven't moved, is it because you're fighting resistance instead of dissolving it?

### Connection to other criteria

Depends on Tactical Empathy (#2) — empathy is the precondition; dissolution is the action. Depends on Force Field Mapping (#8) — you need to know which force is blocking before you can dissolve it. Reinforces Pre-Suasion (#4) — an accusation audit IS a form of pre-suasion (priming them to dismiss their own objections).

### Good example
Before asking a key hire to leave their stable job, you lead with: "You're probably thinking this is too risky, the timing is wrong, and you'd be crazy to walk away from your vesting schedule." They laugh and say "Yeah, basically." Now you can have the real conversation.

### Poor example
You counter every objection with a logical argument. They stop voicing concerns but haven't actually resolved them. They ghost you later.

### Research basis
Chris Voss — *Never Split the Difference* (accusation audit); Miller & Rollnick — *Motivational Interviewing* (rolling with resistance); Bob Moesta — *Demand-Side Sales 101* (anxiety and habit as blocking forces).

## Criterion 10 — Commitment Escalation

**Definition:** Building momentum through a sequence of small, voluntary agreements that make the final ask feel like a natural next step rather than a leap. Each commitment is slightly larger than the last.

**Why it matters:** Cialdini's Consistency principle — people who have made a small commitment are dramatically more likely to agree to a larger one that's consistent with it. The mechanism is identity: once they've said yes to something small, they've self-identified as someone who supports this direction. The subsequent larger ask is consistent with who they've become.

**Impact tier:** Medium (3/5)

**Best direction:** Higher is better.

### Sub-components

- **Foot-in-the-door (Cialdini):** Small public commitment → larger consistent request. Getting commitments in writing increases the effect.
- **Ackerman Method (Voss):** For monetary negotiations: open at 65% of target, increase to 85%, 95%, 100% — each increment smaller than the last. Final number should be precise (non-round) and include a non-monetary bonus.
- **Fogg's Tiny Steps:** Make the first action so small it's almost impossible to refuse. Momentum builds from there.

### Good example
Before asking for a $500K partnership deal, you get them to agree to a joint webinar (tiny commitment), then a co-branded case study (medium), then a pilot project (larger). By the time you propose the full partnership, they've already self-identified as a collaborator.

### Poor example
You go straight for the big ask on the first meeting. Even if they're interested, the gap between "just met you" and "$500K commitment" is too wide to cross in one step.

### Research basis
Robert Cialdini — *Influence* (commitment and consistency); Chris Voss — *Never Split the Difference* (Ackerman method); BJ Fogg — *Tiny Habits*.

# Category 4: Framing & Self-Management

## Criterion 9 — Identity & Unity Alignment

**Definition:** Creating a sense of shared identity ("we") and connecting your ask to how the other person sees themselves or wants to be seen. Leveraging in-group dynamics and self-concept.

**Why it matters:** Cialdini's 7th principle — Unity — is about shared identity, not just similarity. The more someone perceives you as part of "us," the more influence you carry. Beyond Cialdini, identity-based appeals are more durable than incentive-based ones: people don't just agree, they internalize the change as consistent with who they are.

**Impact tier:** Medium (3/5)

**Best direction:** Higher is better.

### Sub-components

- **Unity (Cialdini):** Shared identity > shared similarity. "We're builders" hits harder than "we have similar interests."
- **Self-concept priming (Cialdini Pre-Suasion):** "Are you adventurous?" → they act adventurously. Activate the identity before the ask.
- **Social proof (Cialdini):** "People like you have done X" leverages both identity and conformity.
- **Liking (Cialdini/Carnegie):** Similarity, genuine compliments, cooperation toward mutual goals. People prefer to say yes to those they like.

### Good example
You're recruiting an engineer from big tech. Instead of selling comp and title, you say: "You're the kind of person who builds things from scratch — you've always been a builder, not a maintainer. That's who we need."

### Poor example
You pitch the role based on equity upside and growth trajectory. Good pitch, but it doesn't connect to who they are.

### Research basis
Robert Cialdini — *Influence* (unity, social proof, liking); *Pre-Suasion* (self-concept priming); Dale Carnegie — *How to Win Friends and Influence People*.

## Criterion 11 — Information Control

**Definition:** Strategic management of what information to share, withhold, and when to reveal it. Includes timing of disclosures, controlling the narrative sequence, and ensuring you have more information than the other party.

**Why it matters:** HBS Negotiation's core insight: asymmetric information is competitive advantage. The side that knows more wins. But it's not just about having information — it's about controlling the sequence and timing of reveals to shape the narrative.

**Impact tier:** Medium (3/5)

**Best direction:** Higher is better.

### Sub-components

- **Information asymmetry (HBS):** Research their BATNA, constraints, and pressures before they reveal yours. The side with more information controls the negotiation.
- **Strategic disclosure (HBS/Greene):** Share information that advances your position. Withhold information that weakens it. Never reveal your reservation value.
- **Narrative sequencing (Greene/Cialdini):** The order in which information is presented changes how it's received. Lead with the strongest point. End with the call to action.
- **Contingent proposals (HBS):** "What if we did X?" — reveals their constraints without committing you. Information extraction disguised as brainstorming.

### Good example
In a salary negotiation, you research the market rate, the company's funding stage, and the hiring manager's urgency. You let them name a number first, then deploy your research selectively.

### Poor example
You share everything upfront — your current salary, your target, your other offers — leaving yourself no negotiating room.

### Research basis
HBS Negotiation course (BATNA, ZOPA, information asymmetry, contingent contracts); Robert Greene — *The 48 Laws of Power*.

## Criterion 12 — Emotional Regulation

**Definition:** Managing your own emotional state under pressure so it doesn't contaminate the interaction. Includes Voss's "late-night FM DJ voice," strategic use or suppression of anger, and maintaining composure when the other side escalates.

**Why it matters:** Your emotional state is contagious. Neuroscience research on mirror neurons shows that the calm person in a tense conversation tends to set the emotional tone for everyone else. Voss's "late-night FM DJ voice" — deep, soft, slow, reassuring — soothes the other person's amygdala and creates safety. HBS negotiation research shows anger should only be used deliberately in one-shot interactions; in repeat relationships, it destroys value creation.

**Impact tier:** Medium (3/5)

**Best direction:** Higher is better.

### Sub-components

- **Late-Night FM DJ Voice (Voss):** Deep, slow, downward inflection. Record yourself and listen. The goal is to make the other person's nervous system calm down.
- **Strategic Anger (HBS):** Use anger in one-shot, value-claiming interactions. Suppress it in repeat relationships and value-creating interactions. If you use it, use it deliberately, not reactively.
- **Tactical Silence (Voss):** Pause 4+ seconds after statements. Let the counterpart fill the void. Silence is uncomfortable — they'll often reveal more than they intended.
- **Crucial Conversations Framework (Patterson/Grenny):** When stakes are high, opinions vary, and emotions run strong — make it safe. Mutual purpose + mutual respect = safety. Without safety, people either go to silence (withdrawal) or violence (aggression).

### Good example
In a tense board meeting where a member is challenging your strategy, you slow your voice, lower your pitch, and pause before responding. The calm is contagious — the room de-escalates.

### Poor example
You match their energy, get defensive, and the conversation becomes a contest. Even if you "win" the argument, you lose the room's confidence.

### Research basis
Chris Voss — *Never Split the Difference* (FM DJ voice, tactical silence); HBS Negotiation (strategic use of anger); Patterson, Grenny, et al. — *Crucial Conversations*.
