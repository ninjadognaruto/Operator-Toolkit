# Munger philosophy criteria — complete reference

13 principles for evaluating decisions, strategies, and businesses through Charlie Munger's lens. Each entry includes: definition, why it matters (with Munger's specific framing), impact rating (1-5), what "good" vs "poor" looks like, and sources.

## How to read this reference

- **Impact**: 5 = Critical, 4 = High, 3 = Medium, 2 = Low
- **Best direction**: Higher is always better for these criteria (more alignment with Munger's principles = better)
- **Good example**: A decision or behavior that embodies this principle
- **Poor example**: A decision or behavior that violates it
- **Munger quote**: A direct quote or close paraphrase illustrating the principle

## Part 1: Thinking Quality

### 1. Multidisciplinary mental models
- **Impact**: 4 (High) | **Best**: High (broad latticework)
- **Definition**: Drawing on models from psychology, economics, mathematics, physics, biology, engineering, and accounting rather than relying on a single discipline

- **Why**: Munger's single biggest idea. He advocated building a "latticework" of 80-90 essential models across disciplines, saying "all the wisdom of the world is not to be found in one little academic department." Of those 80-90, "only a mere handful really carry very heavy freight." The failure mode is what he called "man with a hammer" syndrome: "To the man with only a hammer, every problem looks like a nail." Single-discipline experts systematically "torture reality to fit their models."

- **The disciplines and their key models**:
  - **Mathematics/Probability** — compound interest, permutations, combinations, expected value, decision trees. Foundational: "You've got to have models in your head."
  - **Psychology** — Munger's 25 tendencies (see criterion #4). His largest category and the one he considered most underutilized by business professionals.
  - **Microeconomics** — supply/demand, opportunity cost, scale advantages (geometric, distribution, brand, network effects), information asymmetries, incentive structures.
  - **Physics/Engineering** — leverage, critical mass, tipping points, inertia, friction, activation energy, feedback loops. Most reliable because they connect to physical reality.
  - **Biology** — natural selection, adaptation, ecological competition, red queen effects. Maps directly to competitive dynamics.
  - **Accounting** — "the language of practical business life" but with constant recognition of its limits and manipulability.

- **How they interact**: Models work checklist-style across disciplines. When multiple models from different fields converge on the same conclusion, you have high confidence. When they diverge, you have a signal to dig deeper. The convergence of multiple models toward the same conclusion is a "lollapalooza" insight (see criterion #5).

- **Good**: Analyzing a business acquisition using psychology (incentive structures), economics (supply/demand dynamics), biology (competitive ecosystems), and probability (base rates of M&A success) — each lens revealing something the others miss
- **Poor**: Evaluating everything through a single lens — e.g., only financial DCF models, ignoring management psychology and competitive dynamics. "Someone who is really smart but has devoted all of their time to being an expert in a narrow area may be dangerous to themselves and others."
- **Source**: Poor Charlie's Almanack; USC commencement speech 2007; "A Lesson on Elementary Worldly Wisdom" (1994); fs.blog/munger-worldly-wisdom

### 2. Inversion thinking
- **Impact**: 5 (Critical) | **Best**: High (systematically inverts)
- **Definition**: Approaching problems backward — instead of asking "how do I succeed?", asking "what would guarantee failure?" and avoiding those things

- **Why**: "Invert, always invert." Inspired by mathematician Carl Jacobi. Many hard problems become simple when reversed. By identifying what would destroy value, you avoid catastrophic errors that no amount of positive thinking can fix. Munger spent more time identifying what could go wrong than what could go right. This connects to his broader principle that "it is remarkable how much long-term advantage people like us have gotten by trying to be consistently not stupid, instead of trying to be very intelligent." Avoiding big mistakes matters more than finding brilliant moves — and inversion is the primary tool for doing so.

- **Good**: Before launching a product, listing all the ways it could fail (no market need, wrong pricing, bad timing, team gaps) and systematically addressing each; passing on a speculative opportunity with huge upside but existential downside
- **Poor**: Only thinking about upside scenarios; creating optimistic-only projections; never asking "what kills this?"; swinging for fences on every decision without considering tail risks
- **Source**: Central theme across all Munger speeches; inspired by Carl Jacobi; Poor Charlie's Almanack

### 3. Second-order thinking
- **Impact**: 3 (Medium) | **Best**: High (thinks through consequences of consequences)
- **Definition**: Looking beyond the immediate effect of a decision to its downstream and indirect effects

- **Why**: First-order thinking is easy and everyone does it. Competitive advantage comes from asking "and then what?" Munger recognized that most poor decisions come from stopping at the first-order effect and being surprised by the second-order consequences.

- **Good**: Recognizing that cutting prices to gain market share (first-order: more customers) might trigger a price war that destroys industry economics (second-order)
- **Poor**: Offering steep discounts without considering competitor response or margin erosion
- **Source**: Implicit throughout Munger's speeches; explicit in his analysis of business competitive dynamics

## Part 2: Psychology & Bias

### 4. Bias awareness (the 25 tendencies)
- **Impact**: 5 (Critical) | **Best**: High (actively scans for biases)
- **Definition**: Systematically identifying which of Munger's 25 standard causes of human misjudgment might be distorting your judgment — and refusing to let any single ideology, framework, or dogma substitute for this analysis

- **Why**: Munger built a specific, numbered taxonomy in "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment" — not a vague "watch out for biases" warning, but a concrete checklist. He also held an "iron prescription" against ideology: "I feel that I'm not entitled to have an opinion unless I can state the arguments against my position better than the people who are in opposition." Ideology is a specific bias pattern — it replaces thinking with tribal signaling.

- **The complete 25 tendencies**:
  1. **Reward/Punishment Superresponse** — incentive-caused bias (see criterion #6)
  2. **Liking/Loving** — ignoring faults in people/things we love
  3. **Disliking/Hating** — ignoring virtues in people/things we hate
  4. **Doubt-Avoidance** — brain rushes to eliminate uncertainty; dangerous in complex decisions
  5. **Inconsistency-Avoidance** — resisting change to conclusions/habits once formed ("chains too light to be felt until too strong to be broken")
  6. **Curiosity** — the rare tendency that counteracts other biases
  7. **Kantian Fairness** — golden-rule expectations of reciprocal treatment
  8. **Envy/Jealousy** — "the only sin you could never possibly have any fun at"
  9. **Reciprocation** — returning favors/disfavors; exploitable through small gifts or concessions
  10. **Influence-from-Mere-Association** — assuming correlation = causation; high price = quality
  11. **Simple Pain-Avoiding Denial** — distorting reality to escape emotional pain
  12. **Excessive Self-Regard** — overestimating own abilities; endowment effect
  13. **Over-Optimism** — "what a man wishes, he believes"
  14. **Deprival Super-Reaction** — losses hurt ~2x as much as equivalent gains; explains why incumbents fiercely resist disruption
  15. **Social Proof** — thinking/acting like observed peers, especially under stress
  16. **Contrast-Misreaction** — brain registers relative, not absolute values; small incremental changes accumulate unnoticed
  17. **Stress-Influence** — light stress improves performance; heavy stress causes dysfunction
  18. **Availability-Misweighing** — overweighting recent/vivid information (recency bias)
  19. **Use-It-or-Lose-It** — skills atrophy without practice
  20. **Drug-Misinfluence** — chemical dependency destroys cognition
  21. **Senescence-Misinfluence** — cognitive decay with age, partially mitigated by continuous learning
  22. **Authority-Misinfluence** — following leaders blindly, amplified under stress
  23. **Twaddle** — speaking outside competence circle
  24. **Reason-Respecting** — people comply more readily when given reasons, even trivial ones
  25. **Lollapalooza** — multiple tendencies converging (see criterion #5)

- **Most dangerous per Munger**: Incentive-caused bias, inconsistency-avoidance (especially combined with doubt-avoidance), and deprival super-reaction.

- **Good**: Before making a hiring decision, checking whether you're being influenced by liking tendency (charming candidate), authority tendency (impressive resume), or social proof (everyone else likes them); being able to argue the other side of any position you hold
- **Poor**: Making major decisions without awareness of the psychological forces acting on your judgment; filtering all information through a single political or economic lens; dismissing people for belonging to the "wrong" tribe
- **Source**: "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment" (1995 speech, updated 2005); Poor Charlie's Almanack Chapter 11; fs.blog/great-talks/psychology-human-misjudgment

### 5. Lollapalooza detection
- **Impact**: 3 (Medium) | **Best**: High (spots compounding biases)
- **Definition**: Recognizing when 3-5 psychological tendencies, incentives, and social forces converge in the same direction — creating extreme outcomes that are exponential, not additive

- **Why**: Munger's unique contribution to behavioral psychology. He called this the most overlooked insight in the field. Academics study biases in isolation because multi-factor experiments are hard to design in labs. But in the real world, lollapalooza effects are where the real action happens. "When you get two or three of these tendencies operating at the same time in the same direction, you often get a lollapalooza effect." Real-world disasters are almost never single-bias events.

- **Munger's specific examples**:
  - **Open-outcry auctions** — social proof (watching others bid) + commitment/consistency (can't back down) + reciprocity (obligation to continue) + deprival super-reaction (fear of losing) → bids far above rational value
  - **Tupperware parties** — reciprocity (host provided snacks) + liking bias (friendship) + social proof (watching peers buy) + commitment (public statements) → buying things they don't need
  - **Cult indoctrination** — nearly all 25 tendencies at once: isolation, sunk costs, doubt-avoidance, social proof, consistency-avoidance, authority, denial. Munger noted this was the closest to a "full lollapalooza"
  - **Market bubbles** — recency bias + social proof + incentive bias + FOMO (deprival super-reaction) + herd behavior → asset prices detach from reality

- **How to detect**: When ordinary people start making extreme decisions, multiple biases are likely converging. Defense: identify which specific biases are operating, rank by strength, systematically weaken each one.

- **Good**: Recognizing that a hot startup combines social proof (everyone investing), commitment/consistency (you already told people you'd invest), deprival super-reaction (fear of missing out), and authority (celebrity investor leading the round) — a lollapalooza pushing toward a potentially bad decision
- **Poor**: Not noticing when multiple forces push toward the same conclusion; treating convergence as confirmation rather than a warning sign
- **Source**: "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment"; Berkshire annual meetings; fs.blog/great-talks/psychology-human-misjudgment

### 6. Incentive analysis
- **Impact**: 4 (High) | **Best**: High (follows the incentives)
- **Definition**: Understanding that people respond to incentives more reliably than to anything else — and that misaligned incentives explain most institutional dysfunction, including failures of management integrity

- **Why**: Tendency #1 for a reason. Munger admitted that even after a lifetime in the top 5% for understanding incentives, he's surprised every year by how much power he'd previously overlooked. Psychology textbooks are ~1,000 pages but barely cover it. The mechanism is both conscious AND subconscious — a surgeon who removes healthy gallbladders doesn't think he's committing fraud; he's convinced himself the gallbladder is "the source of all medical evil." The cognitive drift is invisible to the person experiencing it.

  This also subsumes management integrity as a separate criterion. Munger's essential filter for management was always about incentive alignment: does the compensation structure reward what you want? Are insiders buying or selling? The question isn't "is management good?" — it's "are management's incentives aligned?"

- **Munger's specific examples**:
  - **FedEx night shift** — planes weren't loading on time because workers were paid hourly (incentivized to work slowly). Switching to per-shift pay — go home when planes are loaded — fixed it immediately.
  - **Surgeons** — fee-for-service creates incentive to operate unnecessarily
  - **Lawyers** — cost-plus billing causes "ghastly abuse" — the more hours billed, the higher the fee
  - **Commission-based advisors** — selling the wrong product because the commission is higher
  - **Xerox** — superior machines sold poorly because salesmen earned bigger commissions on inferior ones
  - **Cash registers** — Munger called their inventor "an effective saint" because they eliminated the incentive to steal

- **The diagnostic**: "Show me the incentive and I will show you the outcome." Before analyzing any problem, ask what incentives are actually in place — not what you want them to be.

- **The antidote**: Design systems where what gets rewarded matches what you actually want to happen.

- **Good**: Before trusting an advisor's recommendation, understanding how they get paid and what they gain from each option; evaluating a CEO by whether their compensation rewards long-term value or short-term revenue
- **Poor**: Trusting that people will act against their financial interests because they "should" or claim to be objective; evaluating management character without examining their incentive structure
- **Source**: "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment" (Tendency #1); Poor Charlie's Almanack; fs.blog/great-talks/psychology-human-misjudgment

### 7. Social proof resistance
- **Impact**: 2 (Low) | **Best**: High (thinks independently of crowds)
- **Definition**: Not doing something just because everyone else is doing it — maintaining independent judgment even when consensus pulls strongly in one direction

- **Why**: "Whenever you find yourself on the side of the majority, it is time to pause and reflect." Social proof is one of the most powerful biases because it feels rational — if everyone smart is doing it, surely it's right? But bubbles, manias, and groupthink all ride on social proof.

- **Good**: Staying out of an overheated market even when colleagues and friends are all making money; being willing to be contrarian when the evidence supports it
- **Poor**: Buying a stock because it's trending on social media; copying a competitor's strategy because "everyone is doing it"
- **Source**: "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment" (Social Proof Tendency); market analysis across Berkshire letters

### 8. Emotional discipline
- **Impact**: 4 (High) | **Best**: High (rational under pressure)
- **Definition**: Maintaining rational judgment during periods of fear, greed, excitement, or loss — not letting emotions override analysis

- **Why**: Munger and Buffett repeatedly emphasized that successful investing requires the "temperament" to sit still when others act impulsively. "The big money is not in the buying and selling, but in the waiting." Most portfolio damage comes from emotional reactions, not analytical errors.

- **Good**: Holding a well-researched position through a market crash because your thesis hasn't changed; not panic-selling
- **Poor**: Selling everything during a market dip out of fear; chasing a hot stock out of excitement; revenge-trading after a loss
- **Source**: Berkshire shareholder meetings; Poor Charlie's Almanack

## Part 3: Business Analysis

### 9. Circle of competence
- **Impact**: 5 (Critical) | **Best**: High (stays within, honest about edges)
- **Definition**: Operating only in areas where you have genuine, deep understanding — honestly knowing where your circle ends and having the intellectual humility to say "I don't know" about everything outside it

- **Why**: "Know the edge of your own competency. It's not a competency if you don't know the edge of it." Operating outside your circle is how smart people make catastrophic mistakes. The circle isn't about being smart — it's about being honest about what you actually understand vs. what you think you understand. This directly embodies Munger's version of intellectual humility: "Knowing what you don't know is more useful than being brilliant." He had far more respect for someone who could say "I don't know" than for someone with a confident answer for everything. The size of your circle matters less than knowing its boundaries.

- **Good**: Warren Buffett avoiding tech stocks for decades because he didn't understand the business models — and being right to do so until he genuinely understood Apple; passing on an investment because you can't understand it, even when others are making money
- **Poor**: A software engineer confidently making biotech investment bets; a marketer making architectural decisions; anyone who says "how hard could it be?"; having confident opinions on every topic; never updating views
- **Source**: Buffett/Munger partnership philosophy; consistent across all Berkshire communications; Poor Charlie's Almanack

### 10. Simplicity
- **Impact**: 3 (Medium) | **Best**: High (simple and understandable)
- **Definition**: Whether a business, strategy, or decision can be understood clearly without requiring heroic assumptions — and having the discipline to put most things in the "Too Hard" pile

- **Why**: Not generic KISS advice. Munger connected simplicity directly to competence, risk, and fraud detection.

  **The Three Baskets**: Yes, No, Too Hard. The key insight: most things go in Too Hard. "If something is too hard to do, we look for something that isn't too hard to do." This isn't laziness — it's discipline. The Too Hard pile removes the pressure to have an opinion on everything, which is where most investors blow up.

  **Simplicity = Competence boundary**: If you can't explain a business simply, you're outside your circle of competence. A small circle understood deeply beats a large circle partially understood.

  **Complexity = Red flag**: "Where you have complexity, by nature you can have fraud and mistakes." Derivatives, securitization, financial engineering — all places where mistakes compound because even the creators don't fully understand what they've built. "I can't think of a single example in my whole life where keeping it simple has worked against us."

  **What they favored**: Coca-Cola, See's Candies, GEICO — businesses where the value proposition is obvious. See's raised prices 10% annually for 40+ years without losing customers. "One-decision businesses" where the competitive position is so clear that future decisions are already determined.

  **What they avoided**: Derivatives ("weapons of mass destruction"), financial engineering, conglomerate structures built on accounting tricks. Anything requiring constant explanation.

- **Good**: "People will always drink Coca-Cola" — a thesis a child could understand; putting a complex fintech play in the Too Hard pile rather than pretending to understand it
- **Poor**: A 50-page financial model with 200 assumptions requiring a PhD to evaluate; a strategy that depends on 12 things going right simultaneously; forcing a decision on something that belongs in Too Hard
- **Source**: Munger's investment philosophy; Berkshire's preference for simple businesses; mastersinvest.com/keep-it-simple; valueinvestingworld.com

### 11. Margin of safety
- **Impact**: 4 (High) | **Best**: High (significant buffer)
- **Definition**: Building in a substantial cushion between your estimate of value and the price you pay — or between your plan and what's needed for it to work

- **Why**: "The margin of safety concept is about making it likely that you have the odds significantly in your favor." You will be wrong sometimes. Your estimates will be off. Unexpected things will happen. The margin of safety is what protects you when reality differs from your expectations. This also embodies Munger's preference for error-avoidance over brilliance — building buffers against your own stupidity is more valuable than optimizing for the best case. Prioritize not losing over winning big.

- **Good**: Only investing when price is 30%+ below estimated intrinsic value; planning a project timeline with 40% buffer; keeping 6 months of expenses in reserve; prioritizing capital preservation over speculative upside
- **Poor**: Paying full price for a business because your model says it's worth exactly that; planning with zero slack; assuming everything will go right; taking asymmetric downside risk for modest upside
- **Source**: Benjamin Graham → Buffett → Munger; "the fourth essential filter"

## Part 4: Decision Process

### 12. Opportunity cost thinking
- **Impact**: 3 (Medium) | **Best**: High (always compares to next best)
- **Definition**: Evaluating every decision not in isolation but against the best available alternative — what are you giving up?

- **Why**: "The wise person asks, 'compared to what?'" Every dollar, hour, or unit of attention spent on one thing is not spent on another. Most people evaluate decisions in isolation (is this good?) instead of comparatively (is this better than the alternative?).

- **Good**: Declining a "good" investment because your existing portfolio already contains better risk-adjusted returns; saying no to a meeting because deep work would be more valuable
- **Poor**: Saying yes to every decent opportunity without comparing it to what else you could do with those resources
- **Source**: Munger's decision-making framework; consistent across Berkshire shareholder communications

### 13. Skin in the game
- **Impact**: 2 (Low) | **Best**: High (aligned personal risk)
- **Definition**: Having personal consequences — financial, reputational, or otherwise — tied to the outcome of your decisions

- **Why**: People make better decisions when they bear the consequences. Munger and Buffett kept virtually all their net worth in Berkshire. When advisors have no skin in the game, their recommendations are structurally suspect.

- **Good**: A fund manager who invests the majority of their own net worth in their fund; a CEO whose compensation is tied to long-term shareholder returns
- **Poor**: Advisors who get paid regardless of outcomes; executives with golden parachutes; consultants who never implement their own recommendations
- **Source**: Berkshire Hathaway shareholder structure; Munger's commentary on aligned incentives
