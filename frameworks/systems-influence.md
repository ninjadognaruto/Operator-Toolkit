# Systems Influence

Rohit's core operating principle here: **most people push the wrong lever.** They push harder on the lever they already have, when the real problem is they're operating at the wrong level of the system entirely.

This skill applies Donella Meadows' leverage point framework to find where to actually intervene — in a system, an organization, a relationship, or a negotiation — before deciding how to act.

---

## The Framework: Leverage Points in Increasing Order of Power

Meadows identified 12 places to intervene in a system, ranked from weakest to most powerful. The higher the number, the lower the leverage:

| Level | Cluster | Category | What It Is |
|-------|---------|----------|------------|
| 12 | Physical Structure | Constants & parameters | Numbers, subsidies, taxes, standards. Easy to change, rarely moves behavior for long |
| 11 | Physical Structure | Buffer sizes | The size of the stock vs. the flows trying to change it (e.g., cash reserves, reputation) |
| 10 | Physical Structure | Stock & flow structures | Physical infrastructure, org charts, process pipelines — hard to change once built |
| 9 | Feedback & Timing | Lengths of delays | Time between action and response. Long delays cause oscillation and misjudgment |
| 8 | Feedback & Timing | Balancing feedback loops | Self-correcting mechanisms that keep a system stable. Weakening these destabilizes |
| 7 | Feedback & Timing | Reinforcing feedback loops | Self-amplifying growth or decline cycles. Find these to accelerate or brake a trend |
| 6 | Information & Rules | Information flows | Who gets what information, when, and in what form. Missing info = missing correction |
| 5 | Information & Rules | Rules of the system | Incentives, punishments, constraints that govern behavior |
| 4 | Purpose & Paradigm | Power to self-organize | Ability to change the system's structure — who can add, remove, or evolve how things work |
| 3 | Purpose & Paradigm | Goals of the system | What the system is actually optimizing for (often not what anyone says it is) |
| 2 | Purpose & Paradigm | Mindset or paradigm | The shared beliefs and assumptions from which the system arises |
| 1 | Purpose & Paradigm | Transcending paradigms | Freedom from attachment to any single worldview — the ability to see the system itself |

**The four clusters:**

1. **Physical Structure** (12–10) — the numbers, buffers, and built infrastructure. Slow to change, low leverage.
2. **Feedback & Timing** (9–7) — delays, balancing loops, and reinforcing loops. Where systems either self-correct or spiral.
3. **Information & Rules** (6–5) — who knows what, and what incentives/constraints govern behavior. Consistently underutilized, high leverage.
4. **Purpose & Paradigm** (4–1) — goals, self-organization, mindset, and the ability to transcend worldviews. Hardest to shift, highest leverage when you can.

**The insight:** Most people live in cluster 1 and wonder why nothing moves. The real work is usually in clusters 2 and 3. Most default interventions live at levels 12–9 — arguments, data, more resources, louder pressure. These rarely produce lasting change because the system restores itself. Real leverage is at levels 6–2: change who knows what, change the rules, change the goal, or change the belief.

## Step 1: Define What You're Trying to Change

Before mapping the system, be precise about the target.

Answer these:
- **What is the system?** An individual, a team, an org, a market, a relationship, a conversation?
- **What's the current behavior?** What is the system actually doing (not what it's supposed to do)?
- **What outcome do you want?** Be specific — not "better alignment" but "team ships on time" or "investor commits by end of quarter."
- **What have you already tried?** Name it explicitly — this reveals which level you've been pushing at.

---

## Step 2: Map the System's Actual Goal

The revealed goal — what the system is *actually* optimizing for — is almost always different from the stated goal. Find it by looking at behavior, not declarations.

Ask:
- What does this system consistently produce? That's what it's optimizing for.
- Who benefits from the current state? That person or group is usually aligned with the system's real goal.
- What would happen if the system changed? Who would lose something?
- What information does the system act on, and what information does it ignore?

> **Example:** A team that consistently misses deadlines but never faces consequences isn't optimizing for speed. It's optimizing for coverage — doing everything so no one can be blamed for missing something. The goal isn't what the roadmap says.

---

## Step 3: Diagnose Where You've Been Pushing

Most failed attempts to change something are failed because the intervention level is too low. Name it.

**Common mismatches:**

- Presenting more data to someone who won't change their mind → you're at level 12 (parameters), but the problem is level 2 (paradigm)
- Repeating the same ask louder or more frequently → still level 12
- Adding a process or deadline → level 10 or 9 (structure/delay), but the real issue may be level 5 (incentives)
- Making a compelling argument → level 6 (information), but if the goal of the system is misaligned, information won't fix it
- Reorganizing a team → level 10 (structure), but if the rules haven't changed, behavior won't either

Ask directly: *At what level have I been pushing? Why hasn't it worked?*

---

## Step 4: Walk Up the Stack to Find the Real Leverage

Starting from where you've been operating, move up the stack and ask: **is this level the source of the resistance?**

Stop at the highest level where you can answer yes — that's where to intervene. Don't skip to level 1 just because it's theoretically most powerful; inaccessible leverage isn't leverage.

**Targeting framework for external influence:**

When the system is a person or group, use this lens to map their level. Rohit's influence model — what people actually care about — maps to Meadows' levels:

| What They Care About | Meadows Level | How to Diagnose |
|---------------------|---------------|-----------------|
| Functional / practical outcomes | 12–9 | What do they explicitly say they need? What metrics are they measured on? |
| Emotional / intangibles | 8–5 | What do they feel protective of? What do they fear losing? What makes them feel heard vs. dismissed? |
| Social / identity / paradigm | 4–1 | How do they want to be seen? What beliefs do they hold about how the world works? What would have to be true for them to change their mind? |

Start with the functional layer to understand the stated problem. Push on the emotional layer when logic isn't landing. Shift the paradigm only when you're willing to invest the relationship capital — it's the most powerful move and the most disruptive.

---

## Step 5: Choose the Intervention

Match the leverage level to a specific action. Be concrete — not "build trust" but "share the information they don't have access to and would change the calculation."

**Check against the change equation before committing:**

> **(Vision × Dissatisfaction) + First Steps > Resistance = Change**

- Is the vision clear and compelling enough? If not, the intervention fails at level 3 (goals).
- Is there enough dissatisfaction with the current state? If not, the system has no reason to move.
- Is the first step small enough to be credible? Big asks without obvious entry points stall.
- What is the resistance made of? Name it — it's usually one of: sunk cost, identity threat, missing information, or misaligned incentives.

If the equation doesn't balance, adjust the intervention before executing.

## Step 6: Output

Produce output in this order:

### The System
One sentence on what the system is and what it's actually optimizing for (the revealed goal, not the stated one).

### Current Intervention Level
What level has been tried. Why it hasn't worked — name the specific reason the system restores to its current state.

### The Real Leverage Point
The highest accessible level where the resistance lives. Name it (e.g., "Level 5 — the incentives don't punish inaction, so there's no pressure to move").

### The Intervention
One specific action. Not a theme — an action. Something that could be executed in the next 7 days.

**Format:**
> "The intervention is [specific action]. This works because it targets [leverage level] by [mechanism]. It shifts [what the system currently does] toward [what you want it to do]."

## When to Move Up the Stack

If an intervention at a given level produces no movement after a reasonable trial:
- It means the system is more resilient at that level than expected — usually because a higher-level force is restoring the behavior
- Move one level up and re-diagnose
- Don't add force at the same level — that's the definition of a losing fight

---

## Rohit's Principles on This

- **Systems don't change because you want them to.** They change when the conditions that sustain the current state are disrupted. Find the conditions first.
- **Stated goals are unreliable.** Look at what the system consistently produces. That's the goal.
- **Information is underrated.** Level 6 is consistently underutilized. Before going to paradigm-shifting arguments, check: does the other party have the information that would change their calculation? Often they don't.
- **Paradigm shifts require narrative, not argument.** You can't logic someone out of a worldview. You show them evidence that their worldview produces the wrong outcomes, and you give them a better story to replace it with.
- **The change equation must balance.** A brilliant intervention targeting the right level still fails if dissatisfaction is too low. Surface the problem before selling the solution.
