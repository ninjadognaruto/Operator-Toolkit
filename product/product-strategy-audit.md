# Product Strategy Audit

Strategy is a set of **prioritized, focused choices** that help you succeed in your mission. It answers: what do we do first (priority), where is our leverage (focus), and what are we explicitly *not* doing (choices). If a document doesn't answer all three, it's not a strategy — it's a plan, a vision, or a task list.

This audit has two parts: the strategy-vs-plan check, then the 10 question pressure test.

---

## Part 1: Strategy vs. Plan Check

Run this first. It takes 60 seconds and catches the most common failure mode.

A real strategy has all four components:

| Component | Definition | Question to ask |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Priority** | What gets done first and why | "Does this give clarity on sequence, or does everything feel equally important?" |
| **Focus** | Where is the point of leverage — your actual strength | "Is this built around a real source of advantage, or just aspiration?" |
| **Choices** | What you are explicitly NOT doing | "Are there things you've named as out of scope? Or is the scope unbounded?" |
| **Win condition** | How success is defined — in business or user outcomes, not outputs | "Is success defined as shipping, or as a measurable outcome?" |

**If it fails the check:** Name which component is missing and ask for it before continuing the audit. A strategy missing choices is a roadmap. A strategy missing a win condition is a vision. Name it clearly — don't dress it up.

## Part 2: The 10 Strategy Questions

Grade each question: ✅ Strong / ⚠️ Weak / ❌ Missing

**A. Does it beat — or create — a market?**
"Will this strategy differentiate meaningfully from what's already being done, or is it playing along with the existing competitive dynamic?" Strategies that are merely "better" in the same category are fragile. Strategies that reframe the category or create a new one are durable.

**B. Is it built on the true source of advantage?**
"Does this strategy tap into what makes you uniquely capable of winning, or is it based on a misdiagnosis of why you earn returns?" Common misdiagnosis: "we have better technology" when the real moat is distribution, data, or supply lock-in.

**C. Is it granular enough about where to compete?**
"Are the markets defined specifically — by customer segment, geography, use case — or are they defined generically?" Broad market definitions fail to allocate resources to match real opportunities. Rohit's rule: if you can't name who specifically you're winning and who you're walking away from, it's not focused enough.

**D. Does it put the company ahead of trends?**
"Is the strategy reactive to today's state of the world, or does it account for how the landscape is shifting?" Identify the 2-3 external trends the strategy bets on. If there are none, the strategy assumes status quo — which is almost always wrong.

**E. Does it embed privileged insight?**
"Is this built on unique data, unique customer relationships, or unique operational learning — or is it common analysis of common data yielding common wisdom?" If a competitor could write the same strategy doc from public information, it's not privileged.

**F. Is uncertainty properly accounted for?**
"Is the strategy assuming the future is knowable, or does it account for genuine uncertainty?" Two failure modes: ignoring uncertainty (overconfident bets) or being paralyzed by it (no bets at all). Good strategy names the key uncertainties and builds in reversible commitments for the highest-uncertainty areas.

**G. Does it balance commitment with flexibility?**
"Are there choices here that create durable commitment (resource allocation, partnerships, architecture decisions), and are there areas preserved for learning and adaptation?" Over-planned strategies become rigid. Under-planned strategies drift. The ratio should be explicit.

**H. Are alternatives evaluated without bias?**
"Has the strategy considered meaningfully different paths and rejected them for real reasons, or did the team converge on this direction without genuine comparison?" Watch for: strategy that looks like the last strategy with a thin veneer of new language, or strategies built around what's easy rather than what's right.

**I. Is there conviction and changed behavior?**
"Does this strategy require the organization to stop doing something it currently does? If not, it's probably not actually a strategy." Real strategy is revealed in reallocation — of budget, people, attention. If nothing stops and nothing changes hands, the strategy is decorative.

**J. Does it translate to clear actions?**
"Is there a direct line from this strategy to specific decisions being made differently next week?" A strategy that doesn't connect to near-term reallocation is a vague statement of intent. It should be possible to name 3-5 concrete decisions this strategy makes differently than the default.

## Part 3: Output Format

After running both parts, produce:

### Strategy Scorecard
```
Component check:
- Priority: ✅ / ⚠️ / ❌
- Focus: ✅ / ⚠️ / ❌
- Choices: ✅ / ⚠️ / ❌
- Win condition: ✅ / ⚠️ / ❌

10 questions: [N/10 strong]
Strongest: [A, C, G]
Weakest: [E, I, J]
```

### The Call
One paragraph. Not a balanced "pros and cons" — a directional verdict. Is this a real strategy or not? What is the single most important thing that would make it stronger?

**Rohit's style:** Be the micro-pessimist here. Healthy skepticism on the day-to-day execution is good. Name what's missing without softening it. The goal is to strengthen the strategy, not to validate it.

### The 2-3 Rewrite Suggestions
Not a full rewrite — name the 2-3 specific changes that would most improve the strategy. Make them operational: "Replace the TAM claim in section 2 with a specific customer segment you're winning and why," not "add more specificity."

## Rohit's Operating Principles on Strategy (embedded reference)

These are Rohit's own principles — apply them as lenses throughout the audit:

- **Ask the right question first.** Go deeper (5 whys) or higher (eigen-question: what's the one question that, if answered, makes all the others easier?). Work backwards from the end goal.
- **Be a micro-pessimist, macro-optimist.** Be visionary about goals, ruthlessly critical about day-to-day execution assumptions.
- **Planning is useful; plans are useless.** The act of strategic thinking matters. The document becomes stale the moment it's written. Does this strategy have mechanisms for learning and revision?
- **The best experience is too much minus one.** When evaluating strategic bets on product experience, ask: what does the 15-star version look like, and how far does this strategy get us there?
