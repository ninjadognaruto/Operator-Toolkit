# Stakeholder Brief

Every audience has three layers of needs — functional, emotional, and social. Most comms only address the functional layer ("here are the facts") and leave the emotional and social layers unaddressed. Those unaddressed layers are why people leave meetings misaligned even when the information was clear.

Rohit's rule: **the PM's job is to own the message.** Not just to share information — to own the narrative, craft the one-line that sticks, and repeat it.

---

## Step 1: Map the Audience's JTBD

Before writing a single word, answer these three questions for the primary audience:

### Functional JTBD
What are they *actually* trying to accomplish in their role right now?
- What metrics are they accountable for?
- What decisions are they trying to make?
- What risks are they trying to manage?
- What does "good" look like for them this quarter?

### Emotional JTBD
How do they want to feel — and what are they afraid of?
- Do they feel heard and included in decisions?
- Are they worried about being surprised or blindsided?
- Do they feel uncertain or in control?
- Common emotional jobs: feel competent, feel like they have visibility, not feel like they're being managed out of a decision

### Social JTBD
How do they want to be perceived by their peers or reports?
- Are they trying to look decisive? Informed? Collaborative?
- Is this communication one they'll forward or reference in their own updates?
- Do they need to be able to explain your decision/work to their own stakeholders?

**The brief should serve all three layers.** A communication that only delivers information (functional) but ignores that the person feels excluded from a decision (emotional) or needs to look informed to their own team (social) will not achieve alignment.

---

## Step 2: Identify the Communication Type

Match the format to the job:

| Type | When to use | Primary job for reader |
|------|------------|----------------------|
| **Exec/board update** | Regular cadence, status + decision | Confidence that things are moving, flag if they need to act |
| **Decision brief** | Asking for approval or alignment | Give them enough to say yes/no without needing more |
| **Investor update** | Monthly/quarterly | Pattern of progress, honest about risks, building trust |
| **Sales enablement** | Equipping sales to message the product | Give them the one-liner + handling the top 3 objections |
| **Partner brief** | Aligning an external partner | Shared interest, clear ask, their win |
| **Team announcement** | Internal, post-decision | What changed, why, what it means for them, what's next |
| **Alignment doc** | Pre-meeting, get people on same page | Reduce meeting time, surface disagreements before the room |

## Step 3: Apply the Message Architecture

Every brief, regardless of length or type, has this structure:

### 1. The Headline (the one thing they should take away)
One sentence. Not a subject line. The actual conclusion or action you need them to hold.

This is Rohit's "own the message" principle in practice. If they remember only one thing, what should it be?

> ❌ "Update on Q3 product work"
> ✅ "We're on track for the October launch, but need a decision on X by Friday to keep the timeline."

### 2. Context (why this matters to them specifically)
1-3 sentences connecting this communication to their actual goals or concerns. Not background for you — signal to them that this is relevant to their world.

### 3. The Core Content
Calibrated to the audience's functional needs:
- Execs and boards: outcomes and risks, not activities. One page or less.
- Investors: metric trajectory + honest narrative + the ask. Signal that you're not hiding.
- Sales teams: the one-liner, the top 3 objections and responses, the proof point.
- Partners: the shared win, the clear ask, the specific next step.
- Internal team: the decision, the reasoning, what changes for them, what stays the same.

### 4. The Ask (or the implied next step)
Make it explicit. What do you need from them, by when?

If there's no ask, say so explicitly: "No action needed — this is for your awareness." Ambiguity about whether a response is expected is the most common reason good communications fail to generate action.

### 5. What You're Not Asking For
Rohit uses this especially in exec and board comms. Naming what is NOT in scope prevents scope creep and keeps the discussion focused.

## Audience-Specific Playbooks

### Board / Investors
- Lead with the headline metric movement (positive or negative — don't bury bad news)
- Three sections: What's working, What's not, What we're doing about it
- Name the key risk explicitly — boards can tell when you're softening, and it erodes trust
- The ask must be specific: a decision, a connection, a piece of advice on a named question
- Tone: confident about direction, honest about uncertainty, not defensive

### Executive stakeholder (your boss, a C-suite partner)
- They don't want to be surprised. Lead with what changed.
- Frame everything in terms of their metrics, not yours
- "This affects [their goal] because [specific reason]"
- Offer a recommendation, not just a problem
- One page. If they need more, they'll ask.

### Sales team
- They need the one-liner they can say in 30 seconds, not a feature list
- Give them: the positioning one-liner → the top 3 customer objections → how to handle each → one proof point per objection
- Capabilities and benefits, not comparatives (see positioning-canvas skill)
- Make it easy to repeat — not to understand, to repeat

### External partner
- Lead with their win, not yours
- What does success look like from their side?
- Be explicit about the ask and the timeline
- Name the one thing you need them to do — not five things

### Internal team (announcement, pivot, decision)
- People want: what's changing, why, what it means for me, what's next
- Give them reasons, not just decisions — they'll fill the silence with their own interpretation
- Acknowledge what's hard about it if something is hard
- "What stays the same" is often as important as "what changes"

## Style Rules (Rohit's voice in communications)

- **Lead with the conclusion.** Don't build to the point — start with it.
- **One paragraph, one idea.** Don't combine the update with the ask with the context in one block.
- **Short sentences in important moments.** Long sentences signal uncertainty. Short sentences signal confidence.
- **No weasel words.** "We believe," "we think," "it seems" — cut them. State the thing.
- **Name the risk before they do.** Proactively naming a risk builds more trust than hoping they don't notice it.
- **Repeat the headline.** End where you started. The last thing they read is the thing they remember.
