# Hard Decision Framework

Rohit's operating principle on decisions: **the only way out is through.** Deferring is almost always worse than deciding wrong and learning fast. The goal of this framework is not to find the "right" answer — it's to make the best call with available information, name the key risks explicitly, and build in the right checkpoints to know if you're wrong.

He uses inversion as his primary move. Most decision tools start with "what do I want?" — this one starts with the opposite.

---

## Step 1: Reframe the Question (Inversion)

Before analyzing anything, flip the question. Inversion reveals what "want" thinking hides.

Run all four flips:

| Original Question | Inverted Version |
|------------------|-----------------|
| What do I want? | What will I regret not having in 5 years? |
| What does [other party] want? | What do they regret not having — what are they motivated by out of envy, not just stated preference? |
| Should we do this? | What's the worst that could happen — and is it recoverable? |
| Why should I do this? | Who else would do this if not me, and why wouldn't they? |

**The inversion test often resolves the decision immediately.** If the inverted version of the question reveals that the downside is unrecoverable or that you have no genuine edge, the answer is usually no. If it reveals that the regret of *not* doing it is higher than the risk of doing it, the answer is usually yes.

---

## Step 2: Apply the Micro-Pessimist / Macro-Optimist Filter

Rohit's mental model: be visionary about goals and genuinely skeptical about near-term execution assumptions.

**Macro (the vision):** Is the goal worth pursuing at all? Does it move the needle on something that matters? Is the upside real and meaningful?

**Micro (the execution):** What are the 3 most likely ways this goes wrong in the next 90 days? Not worst-case scenarios — the *most likely* failure modes. Name them specifically.

This prevents two common failure modes:
- Being too pessimistic at the macro level (killing good ideas before they start)
- Being too optimistic at the micro level (assuming execution will be smooth)

---

## Step 3: Classify the Decision Type

Different decisions need different approaches:

**Type 1 — Reversible with low cost:**
- What to build next, how to frame a pitch, which experiment to run
- Rule: Decide fast. The cost of a wrong decision is low; the cost of delay is real.
- Don't spend more than 20 minutes on Type 1 decisions.

**Type 2 — Reversible with high cost:**
- Hiring, pricing changes, partnership terms, product architecture
- Rule: Gather signal quickly, set a decision date, decide. Don't let "gathering more information" become a proxy for avoidance.
- Typical timeline: 1-2 weeks with structured inputs.

**Type 3 — Irreversible:**
- Fundraising terms, co-founder agreements, pivots, shutdowns, major strategic bets
- Rule: Apply all steps of this framework fully. Explicitly name what makes it irreversible. Separate the decision from urgency (urgency is often manufactured).
- One question to ask: "What would I need to see to confidently reverse this?" If there is no good answer, it's truly irreversible.

---

## Step 4: The Commitment Test

Rohit's principle: **100% or don't commit at all.** Half-commitment is the worst outcome — it consumes resources without generating real learning or real results.

Before committing to a path, answer:

1. "Am I willing to give this 100% for the required time period?"
2. "Am I committing because I believe in this, or because committing feels less uncomfortable than deciding?"
3. "If this fails, will I be able to say I gave it everything — or will I always wonder if more effort would have changed it?"

If the answer to #1 is no, that's a decision: don't do it.

---

## Step 5: The Edge Check

*Why should I do this? Who else would do this if not me?*

This is Rohit's version of the unfair advantage test. For any significant commitment:
- What do you know or have that makes you better positioned than the default alternative?
- If the answer is "nothing specific," the expected value of doing this vs. not doing this is roughly equal — and uncertainty should break toward the simpler path.
- If the answer is clear and specific, that's your signal.

---

## Output Format

Produce the output in this order:

### The Decision
State the decision clearly in one sentence. If it's ambiguous, name that — clarity about the question is half the work.

### Inversion Results
For each of the 4 inversions: what does flipping the question reveal? 1-2 sentences per flip.

### Macro / Micro Assessment
- Macro: [Is the vision sound? Yes/No + one sentence]
- Micro risks: [3 most likely execution failure modes, labeled by probability: likely / possible / unlikely]

### Decision Type
[Type 1 / 2 / 3 — with the implication for how fast to decide]

### The Call
One paragraph. Rohit wants a recommendation, not a menu of options. Name the call, name the key assumption it depends on, and name the earliest checkpoint where you'd know if the assumption was wrong.

**Format:**
> "My call is [X]. This depends on [key assumption]. The earliest signal that this is wrong would be [leading indicator], which we'd see by [timeframe]. If we see that signal, the right move is [course correction]."

### Commitment Check
[Is this 100% or not? What would 100% actually look like in practice?]

## Rohit's Decision Principles (embedded reference)

- **Planning is useful; plans are useless.** Commit to the direction, stay flexible on the execution path.
- **Think in chapters.** This decision is one chapter, not the whole book. Work backwards from the end of the chapter, not from the current moment.
- **It is more important to do minor things many times than to do a big thing once.** When choosing between a bold bet and a series of experiments, default to experiments unless the bold bet is truly irreversible.
- **"You miss 100% of the shots you don't take."** When the decision is between doing something and doing nothing, the inaction bias is real — name it.
- **Earn your luck.** The decision calculus should include: does this put me in a position where luck can find me? Optionality has value.
- **Pygmalion effect on goal-setting.** The ambition of the goal changes the energy brought to it. Don't sandba the goal to protect yourself from failure.
