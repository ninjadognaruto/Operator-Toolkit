# Positioning Canvas

Positioning is not your tagline. It is the internal strategic decision about what game you're playing, who you're playing it for, and what makes you the obvious choice in that game. Everything downstream — messaging, sales pitch, feature prioritization, even hiring — follows from positioning.

Rohit's rule: **Own the one-line that describes your product and solution. Repeat it again and again.** If your team can't say it without looking at a doc, the positioning isn't set yet.

---

## The Framework: 6 Components in Order

Work through these in sequence. Earlier answers constrain later ones — don't skip ahead.

### Component 1: Competing Alternatives
*What would the customer do if your product didn't exist?*

This is not "our competitors." It is everything the customer might use to solve the problem instead:
- Direct competitors (similar products)
- Indirect alternatives (spreadsheets, manual processes, hiring someone, doing nothing)
- The status quo (inertia — the most powerful competitor of all)

For B2B specifically: **the null hypothesis is always that they will do nothing.** Your real competition is often "we'll handle it with the existing process."

List 3-5 real alternatives. Be honest. If you can't name what you're replacing, you haven't found the real problem.

---

### Component 2: Unique Attributes (vs. those alternatives)
*What do you do that the alternatives cannot or do not?*

These should be specific, defensible, and grounded in reality — not marketing language.
- Feature-level differences (specific capabilities)
- Delivery model differences (SaaS vs. on-premise, real-time vs. batch, self-serve vs. managed)
- Structural differences (data advantages, network effects, integration depth)
- Service/experience differences (time to value, support model, onboarding)

**Rohit's test:** Could a competitor truthfully say the same thing? If yes, it's not a unique attribute — it's table stakes.

---

### Component 3: Value Themes
*Group your unique attributes into 2-3 themes that explain the value, not the feature.*

Map each attribute to a value theme: Why does this matter to the customer? What outcome does it drive?

The best themes focus on **capabilities and benefits**, not comparatives.
- ❌ "50% faster than alternative X"
- ✅ "Leave your charger at home" (battery life benefit, not comparison)
- ❌ "More accurate than manual processes"
- ✅ "Decisions your team can act on immediately, not data they have to clean first"

Aim for 2-3 themes. More than 3 and you've lost focus.

---

### Component 4: Target Customer (High-Expectation Customer)
*Who is the customer that would be most disappointed if your product didn't exist?*

This is not the largest segment or the easiest segment. This is the customer for whom your unique attributes create the most disproportionate value. They are:
- Experiencing the problem acutely, frequently
- Underserved by current alternatives
- Willing to change behavior and pay for a better solution

For B2B, map this across the buying committee:
- **Economic buyer:** Who approves spend? What are their functional, emotional, social jobs?
- **Champion:** Who advocates internally? What is their personal win?
- **End user:** Who uses it daily? What does friction cost them?

**Rohit's B2B filter:** The best products result in the customer either getting promoted, contributing to their revenue, or not getting fired. Which of the three does yours serve?

---

### Component 5: Market Frame of Reference
*What category do you place yourself in — and does that category make your strengths the most relevant?*

The market frame is the context you give buyers to understand what you are. It determines what they compare you to and what success looks like.

Choosing the right frame is strategic, not descriptive:
- A narrow frame ("expense management for distributed teams") makes you the obvious choice for a specific problem but limits TAM
- A broad frame ("financial operations platform") captures more budget but invites comparison to well-established players
- A new frame ("revenue intelligence") can create a category but requires more education

**Test:** Does your chosen frame put your unique attributes at the center of the evaluation criteria? If evaluators in your frame compare you on dimensions where you're not differentiated, change the frame.

---

### Component 6: The Positioning Statement
*Assemble the components into a statement and a one-liner.*

**Full positioning statement:**
> For [target customer] who [need or situation], [product name] is a [market frame] that [key benefit]. Unlike [competing alternative], [product name] [primary differentiator].

**One-liner (the test of real positioning):**
> [Product name] helps [specific customer] [functional job] without [the painful tradeoff they currently accept].

Rohit's standard: if a prospect hears the one-liner and immediately says "tell me more" or "we have that problem," the positioning is working. If they nod politely, it's not landing.

## The Positioning Audit (if reviewing existing positioning)

Run these questions against current positioning:

1. **Alternatives test:** Is it clear what this replaces? Can a buyer immediately understand what they'd stop using?
2. **Differentiation test:** Is there at least one claim that a direct competitor could not truthfully make?
3. **Benefit test:** Is the value expressed as an outcome the customer cares about, or a feature the team is proud of?
4. **Customer test:** Is there a specific type of customer named, or is it "any company that..."?
5. **Frame test:** Does the market frame give us favorable comparison criteria?
6. **Repeatability test:** Can every person on the GTM team say the one-liner without looking at a doc?

Score each: ✅ Strong / ⚠️ Weak / ❌ Missing

## Rohit's Principles on Messaging

- Own the message. The PM's job in GTM is to own the one line that describes the product — and repeat it everywhere until everyone inside and outside the company knows it.
- **Don't use comparatives in feature descriptions.** "Better," "faster," "clearer" invite the customer to benchmark against you. Capabilities and benefits ("leave your charger at home") let the customer picture their own life improved.
- PMs should be in the middle of sales ↔ marketing, not just design ↔ engineering. Positioning is a product decision, not just a marketing one.
